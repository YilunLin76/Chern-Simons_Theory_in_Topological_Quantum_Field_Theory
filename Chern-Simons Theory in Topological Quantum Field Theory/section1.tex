\subsection{Overview of Topological Quantum Field Theory} % 3~5 pages
\setstretch{1.2}
\par Following a protracted, intricate, and multi-threaded evolution from the 1930s through the 1970s, quantum field theory culminated in an unprecedented and universal success in applying field theory methods to develop relativistic quantum theory, providing an exceedingly precise picture to describe the electromagnetic force, the weak interaction, and the strong nuclear force down to a remarkably microscopic scale\,\cite{wilczek1999}. Via the key theoretical advances of renormalization and gauge field theory during the 1940s to 1960s, the theoretical framework of standard model was yielded, which consists of QED (in the late 1940s), the Glashow-Weinberg-Salam Theory (in the 1960s), and QCD (in the 1970s). \par Despite its tremendous success in describing the other fundamental interactions, the framework of quantum field theory encountered profound and seemingly insurmountable obstacles in the quantization of gravity. At the Planck length, the gravitational interaction becomes overwhelmingly significant and could no longer be ignored. However, since all models in quantum field theory depend on a specific and fixed spacetime background--the Minkowski spacetime, the violent fluctuations of spacetime at such a tiny scale ($10^{-35}$m) obscure many foundational concepts in quantum field theory, and lead to severe deviations in theoretical predictions. Although Einstein's general relativity is in precise agreement with existing experimental results, a wealth of theoretical motivations suggest that general relativity must be replaced or embedded within a more fundamental quantum theory of gravity beyond its present range of validity\,\cite{keifer2006}. These evidences imply that QFT is only a theory emerging as a limiting case at a scale where the gravity is negligible; and the resolution of these difficulties seems impossible to be found in quantum field theory itself at all\,\cite{gross1999}. These problems have been realized by the community for a very long time, even since the inception of quantum field theory. Coincident with the establishment of quantum field theory in the last century, theoretical physicists also explored multiple alternative approaches to the quantization of gravity. To build up these approaches, physicists need a strong intuition in abstract mathematics such as algebraic topology and differential geometry. One of the approaches is topological quantum field theory, where topological invariants can be used to build up a metric-independent quantum field theory.
\par The intellectual roots of topological quantum field theory lie in geometric analysis. The Reidemeister torsion $\tau$, a combinatorial manifold invariant, has long been established by mathematicians as a refined topological invariant capable of distinguishing spaces that are homotopy equivalent but not simple homotopy equivalent. Without going into detail, given a chain complex $C=(C_n,d_n)$, $\mathbf{u}_i$ are some bases for $C_i$\,; $\mathbf{v}_i$ are some bases for $H_i(C)$, the homologies of $C$\,; $\mathbf{w}_i$ are some bases for the i-boundaries $im(d_{i+1})$. Then the Reidemeister torsion $\tau$ of the chain complex $C$ is defined by
\begin{equation}\label{eq: R-torsion of chain complex}
    \begin{split}
        \log{\tau (C)}\,=\,\sum_{i=0}^{n} \,(-1)^i\,\log\left[ \mathbf{w}_i,\mathbf{v}_i,\mathbf{w}_{i-1}/\mathbf{u}_i\right],
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
where $\left[\mathbf{x}/\mathbf{y}\right]$ denotes the determinant of the matrix which transforms the vector $\mathbf{y}$ to the vector $\mathbf{x}$\,\cite{milnor1966}. 
\par Building upon the framework of Reidemeister torsion, Ray and Singer introduced the notion of analytic torsion in 1971, with the aim of establishing a deep connection between topology and analysis. It was observed that, through a sequence of rigorous and subtle mathematical manipulations, the Reidemeister torsion, as defined in \eqref{eq: R-torsion of chain complex}, could be reformulated as a function of the orthogonal representation of the fundamental group of the manifold, in the form of
\begin{equation}\label{eq: R-torsion of representation}
    \begin{split}
        \log{\tau_M (X)}\,\equiv\,\log{\tau\left(C\left(M,X\right)\right)}\,=\,
        \log{\left[\prod_{j=1}^{n+1}\,\sqrt{\det(\mathbf\Delta_{j-1})}^{(-1)^j\cdot(j-1)}\right]},
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
given $M$ is a finite-cell complex whose cellular chain complex is $C$ while $X$ is an orthogonal representation of the fundamental group of $M$. In \eqref{eq: R-torsion of representation}, $\mathbf{\Delta}_j$ denotes the coordinate representation of the combinatorial Laplacian $\mathbf{\Delta}$ with respect to a given basis on $C_j$, which can be defined in terms of the boundary operator $\mathcal{B}$ on $C$ as
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \mathbf{\Delta}\,&\equiv\,\,\mathcal{B}\,\mathcal{B}^T\,+\,\mathcal{B}^T\,\mathcal{B}, \\
        \mathbf{\Delta}\,&\rightarrow\,\mathbf{\Delta}_j\,=\,\{\Delta_j^{\mu\nu}\}.
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
The core idea of the above reformulation \eqref{eq: R-torsion of representation} is replacing the algebraic dependence on the specific choice of bases with the combinatorial Laplacian, whose spectrum encapsulates the same information in a coordinate-free manner. This remarkable observation then naturally paves the way for a generalization of the concept of analytic torsion $T$, with the application of the spectral zeta function $\zeta_k$ of the analytic Hodge Laplacian $\mathbf{\Delta}^{Hodge}$\,\cite{raySinger71rtorsion},\,\cite{raySinger1973Analytic}. In detail, the Hodge Laplacian (also known as the de Rham Laplacian) is a fundamental concept in Riemannian geometry, which is defined in terms of the exterior derivative $d:\,\Omega^j\left(N\right)\rightarrow\Omega^{j+1}\left(N\right)$ and its codifferential $\delta:\,\Omega^j\left(N\right)\rightarrow\Omega^{j-1}\left(N\right)$ on a Riemannian manifold $\left(N,g\right)$, in the form of 
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \mathbf{\Delta}^{Hodge}&:\,\Omega^j\left(N\right)\rightarrow\,\Omega^j\left(N\right),\\
        \mathbf{\Delta}^{Hodge}\,&\equiv\,d\circ\delta\,+\delta\circ d.
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
And the spectral zeta function $\zeta_k\left(r\right)$ of $\mathbf{\Delta}^{Hodge}$ is defined as the sum of the negative-$r$ powers of the nonzero eigenvalues of $\mathbf{\Delta}_k^{Hodge}$, which is the matrix representation of $\mathbf{\Delta}^{Hodge}$ with respect to a given basis for each space $\Omega^k\left(N\right)$ of smooth differential $k$-forms on the Riemannian manifold $\left(N,g\right)$. The derivative of this spectral zeta function at $r=0$ is used to construct the $\zeta$-regularized determinant of the Hodge Laplacian, as follows:
\begin{equation}\label{eq: regularized determinant of the Hodge Laplacian}
    \begin{split}
        \mathrm{det}^{(reg)}\left(\mathbf{\Delta}_k^{Hodge}\right)\,\equiv\,\exp\left[\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\zeta_k\left(r\right)\right|_{r=0}\right].
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
With the substitutional relationship embedded in \eqref{eq: regularized determinant of the Hodge Laplacian}, and in analogy with definition \eqref{eq: R-torsion of representation}, it can be deduced that the logarithm of the determinant of Hodge Laplacian in the expression of the logarithm of the torsion can be replaced with the value of the derivative of the spectral zeta function at $r=0$, leading to the definition of analytic torsion:
\begin{equation}\label{eq: spectral definition of analytic torsion}
    \begin{split}
        \log{T}\,=\,\sum_{k=1}^{n+1}\,\frac{\left(k-1\right)\cdot\left(-1\right)^{k+1}}{2}\cdot\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\zeta_k\left(r\right)\right|_{r=0}.
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
The equivalence between the analytic torsion and the Reidemeister torsion was rigorously proved by Cheeger and M{\"u}ller and is encapsulated in the Cheeger-M{\"u}ller theorem\,\cite{cheeger1979AnalyticTorsion},\,\cite{muller1978AnalyticRTorsion}. 
\par The definition \eqref{eq: spectral definition of analytic torsion} of analytic torsion through the spectral zeta function played an important role in constructing the first example of a topological quantum field theory, also known as the Schwarz-type TQFT\,\cite{ASchwarz1979}. At the core of this construction lies the insight that analytic torsion can be formulated as the partition function (a functional integral) associated with a degenerate quadratic functional (the action)\,\cite{SchwarzQFTTopology}. 
\par A detailed mathematical exposition is required to elucidate the rationale underlying this core idea. Firstly, we know that the Hodge Laplacian defined on the space of smooth $q$-forms of a compact Riemannian manifold is a positive-definite and self-adjoint operator acting on an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space. While it is well known that, in linear algebra, the Gaussian integral associated with such a positive-definite and self-adjoint operator in a finite-dimensional space has the form of
\begin{equation}\label{eq: Gaussian integral in finite-dimensional case}
    \begin{split}
        \int_{\mathbb{R}^n}e^{-\frac{1}{2}x^{T}Ax}\,\mathrm{d}x\,=\,\sqrt{\frac{\left(2\pi\right)^n}{\det A}}\,\propto\,\left(\det A\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}. 
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
By analogy with the finite-dimensional case, while the dimension of the space tends to infinity, it is expected that the Gaussian integral of a positive-definite and self-adjoint operator, as is the case for the Hodge Laplacian $\mathbf{\Delta}^{Hodge}$, takes the form:
\begin{equation}\label{proportionality between Gaussian integral determinant^-1/2}
    \begin{split}
        \int_{\mathscr{H}} \mathcal{D}\phi\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}\langle\phi,\mathbf{\Delta}^{Hodge}\phi\rangle}\,\propto\,\left(\det\mathbf{\Delta}^{Hodge}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}},
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
where $\mathscr{H}$ denotes the infinite-dimensional Hilbert space. This conclusion \eqref{proportionality between Gaussian integral determinant^-1/2} already implies that the analytic torsion, to some extent, exhibits a robust interior correlation with the Gaussian integral. That is because according to \eqref{eq: spectral definition of analytic torsion} and the relationship in \eqref{eq: regularized determinant of the Hodge Laplacian}, the logarithm of the analytic torsion is expressed as a superposition of logarithms of $\left(\det\mathbf{\Delta}^{Hodge}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}$ which is directly proportional to the Gaussian integral. 
\par To furthermore reformulate the analytic torsion as a functional integral in a rigorous form based on \eqref{eq: regularized determinant of the Hodge Laplacian}, \eqref{eq: spectral definition of analytic torsion} and \eqref{proportionality between Gaussian integral determinant^-1/2}, we need to consider the integral corresponding to the Hodge Laplacian $\mathbf{\Delta}^{Hodge}_{j}$ on each space $\Omega^{j}\left(M\right)$ of smooth differential $j$-forms, which might take the form:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \mathcal{Z}_{j}\,\equiv\,\int_{\Omega^{j}\left(M\right)}\mathcal{D}\phi_{j}\,\cdot\,\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\langle\phi_{j},\mathbf{\Delta}^{Hodge}_{j}\phi_{j}\rangle\right),
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
hence
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \mathcal{Z}_{j}\,\propto\,\left(\det\mathbf{\Delta}^{Hodge}_{j}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}.
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
On the basis of this definition of $\mathcal{Z}_{j}$, the expression
\begin{equation}\label{eq: product of partition functions}
    \begin{split}
        \mathcal{Z}\,\equiv\,\prod_{j=0}^{n}\mathcal{Z}_{j}^{j\,\cdot\,\left(-1\right)^{j}}
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
of a product of Gaussian integrals raised to different powers provides a partition function which is proportional to the analytic torsion $T$ formulated by Ray and Singer\,\cite{ASchwarz1979}. That is to say, the logarithm of analytic torsion is equivalent to the logarithm of the partition function of a degenerate quadratic action. 
\par However, due to the fact that the Laplacian $\mathbf{\Delta}^{Hodge}$ has a nontrivial kernel, that is, it possesses eigenvalues which are zero, its determinant vanishes, causing the corresponding Gaussian integral to diverge. This indicates that the determinant in its conventional form
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \det\mathbf{\Delta}^{Hodge}\,=\,\prod\lambda_i
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
cannot be straightforwardly employed to express the Gaussian integral. Furthermore, the same methodology pioneered by Ray and Singer can be applied here to address the issue of divergences. In the following, a more detailed explanation of why this method of regularization is effective for the Hodge Laplacian defined on an infinite-dimensional Hilbert space is provided. Consider the case of a finite-dimensional space, where we could also define the spectral zeta function for a positive-definite and self-adjoint operator $A$ as
\begin{equation}\label{eq: definition of zeta function}
    \begin{split}
        \zeta_{A}\left(r\right)\,\equiv\,\sum\lambda_{i}^{-r}.
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
Thus
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \zeta_{A}'\left(r\right)\,\equiv\,\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}r}\zeta_{A}\left(r\right)\,=\,-\sum\lambda_{i}^{-r}\cdot \ln\left(\lambda_{i}\right),
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \zeta_{A}'\left(0\right)\,=\,-\sum\ln\left(\lambda_{i}\right)\,=\,-\ln\prod\lambda_{i}\,=\,-\ln\left(\det A\right).
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
That means, by saying
\begin{equation}\label{traditional interpretation of Gaussian integral}
    \begin{split}
        \int_{\mathbb{R}^n}e^{-\frac{1}{2}x^{T}Ax}\mathrm{d}x\,\propto\,\left(\prod\lambda_{i}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
about the Gaussian integral associated with the operator $A$, we are actually also implying
\begin{equation}\label{essence of Gaussian integral in finite-dimensional case}
    \begin{split}
        \int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-\frac{1}{2}x^{T}Ax}\mathrm{d}x\,\propto\,\left(e^{-\zeta_{A}'\left(0\right)}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}.
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
And this \eqref{essence of Gaussian integral in finite-dimensional case} is precisely the mathematical essence of the statement \eqref{eq: Gaussian integral in finite-dimensional case}. 
\par In the case of a finite-dimensional operator, statements \eqref{traditional interpretation of Gaussian integral} and \eqref{essence of Gaussian integral in finite-dimensional case} are mathematically equivalent. However, in the case for an positive-definite operator on the infinite-dimensional Hilbert space, where zero modes could lead to the determinant vanishing, this mathematical essence \eqref{essence of Gaussian integral in finite-dimensional case} plays a vital role in regularizing the determinant and deriving the correct integral. To this end, we just need to simply exclude the contribution of the zero modes from the expression on the right-hand side of \eqref{essence of Gaussian integral in finite-dimensional case}\,\cite{raySinger71rtorsion}. That is, for an infinite-dimensional operator, such as $\mathbf{\Delta}^{Hodge}$, replace the definition \eqref{eq: definition of zeta function} with $\zeta_{\mathbf{\Delta}^{Hodge}}\left(r\right)\,\equiv\,\sum_{nonzero}\lambda_{i}^{-r}$ which does not include any of the operator's zero eigenvalues $\lambda_{i}\,=\,0$. After refining this definition, the above essence \eqref{essence of Gaussian integral in finite-dimensional case} would still naturally remain valid, following the same procedure of derivation as in the finite-dimensional case. Actually, this invariance of the expression \eqref{essence of Gaussian integral in finite-dimensional case} is the reason why  we consider it to be the essence underlying the relationship \eqref{traditional interpretation of Gaussian integral}. 
\par Therefore, now we have 
\begin{equation}\label{proportionality of infinite-dimensional Gaussian integral}
    \begin{split}
        \int_{\mathscr{H}}\mathcal{D}\phi\,e^{-\frac{1}{2}\langle\phi,\mathbf{\Delta}^{Hodge}\phi\rangle}\,\propto\,\left(e^{-\zeta_{\mathbf{\Delta}^{Hodge}}'\left(0\right)}\right)^{-\frac{1}{2}}
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
for the Hodge Laplacian acting on infinite-dimensional Hilbert space. Incidentally, the proportionality constant associated with \eqref{proportionality of infinite-dimensional Gaussian integral} depends heavily on the spatial measure $\mathcal{D}\phi$ corresponding to the functional integral, although evidently, $\mathcal{D}\phi$ is only a formal integration measure in physics whose normalization could not be rigorously determined in mathematics, just as in many well known constructions in fundamental quantum field theory. 
\par The above derivation from \eqref{eq: definition of zeta function} to \eqref{proportionality of infinite-dimensional Gaussian integral} is how the methodology of zeta function pioneered by Ray and Singer is successfully applied to address the divergent integral. 
\par This partition function \eqref{eq: product of partition functions} associated with analytic torsion, along with the technique of $\zeta$-function regularization of determinants, constitutes the cornerstone of Schwarz's construction of the first topological quantum field theory during 1978-1979. It is important to note that the degenerate action established via analytic torsion is independent of the choice of metric on the manifold, which is a feature that aligns naturally with the topological nature of TQFT\,\cite{Schwarz1978}. 
\par Formulating a metric-independent action is far from sufficient for establishing a complete theoretical framework of topological quantum field theory and providing a deep insight into the philosophical ideas underlying the paradigm of TQFT. A full development of the theory requires further investigation into the construction of concrete and computable exemplary theories. BF theory and Chern-Simons theory are both such theories that have been studied extensively in physics community. 
\par The main body of this paper is devoted to a detailed discussion of the technical underpinnings of Chern-Simons theory, tracing its step-by-step development within mathematics and its formulation as a functor in topological quantum field theory. Chern-Simons theory plays a pivotal role in bridging the initial heuristic ideas proposed by Schwarz and the subsequent rigorous axiomatic system introduced by Atiyah. Through the synthesis of Chern-Simons theory with the foundational idea of path integral in the physical paradigm, the field has achieved a profound understanding of the mathematical structures underlying this metric-independent theory. 

\subsection{Historical Background of Chern-Simons Theory} % 2~3 pages
\setstretch{1.2}
\par Chern-Simons theory has had a profound impact on theoretical physics. However, it was originally developed independently within the realm of pure mathematics.
\par To appreciate the historical foundations and conceptual precursors of Chern-Simons theory, one must trace back to the formulation of Chern-Weil theory in the 1940s. From 1944 on, S. S. Chern embarked on a systematic exploration of the formulation of characteristic classes through the use of connections and curvature forms. Through the collaboration with A. Weil, Chern further developed a rigorous framework for constructing characteristic classes of principal bundles by expressing them as de Rham cohomology classes derived from invariant polynomials in curvature forms. In a more precise manner, the Chern-Weil theorem clearly indicates that invariant polynomials applied to curvature forms yield differential forms that represent topological invariants of smooth principal bundles\,\cite{chern1946characteristic}. This central idea serves as the cornerstone that shapes the development of the Chern-Weil theory, which establishes an insightful and elegant connection between differential geometry and topological invariants. Chern-Weil theory significantly contributes to the later emergence of Chern-Simons theory.
\par Building upon a profound understanding of geometric objects such as connections, curvature, and characteristic classes, Chern continued carefully studying the conceptual structures and gradually developed Chern-Simons theory as a natural extension of Chern-Weil theory in the 1970s. In Chern-Weil theory, the differential form representatives of characteristic classes depend on the particular choice of connection, although they represent the same cohomology class in the de Rham cohomology. Chern and Simons attempted to attain a deeper understanding of the geometric structure underlying the differences among representatives of characteristic classes arising from distinct connections. This line of inquiry laid the groundwork for the development of secondary characteristic classes. 
\par We now provide a somewhat rigorous mathematical description to the main idea of Chern-Simons theory which is briefly stated in the previous paragraph. Suppose we are given two different connections $A_{0}$ and $A_{1}$ on the same principal bundle, $f$ is an invariant polynomial acting on the curvature space of connections. Applying $f$ on the curvature $F$ gives a closed differential form $f\left(F\right)$, whose de Rham conjugacy class represents a topological invariant. Let $F_{0}$ and $F_{1}$ denote the curvatures of connections $A_{0}$ and $A_{1}$ respectively, that is,
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        F_{0}\,&=\,\mathrm{d}A_{0}\,+\,A_{0}\wedge A_{0} \\
        F_{1}\,&=\,\mathrm{d}A_{1}\,+\,A_{1}\wedge A_{1}.
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
Although $f\left(F_{0}\right)$ and $f\left(F_{1}\right)$ represent the same characteristic class, they differ in form. Furthermore, the difference of them is an exact form, which means
\begin{equation}\label{eq: difference of invariant polynomials}
    \begin{split}
        f\left(F_{1}\right)-f\left(F_{0}\right)\,=\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{Q}_{2n-1}\left(A_{0},A_{1}\right).
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
When Chern and Simons studied this differences of characteristic class in 1974, they systematically constructed the differential form $\mathbf{Q}_{2n-1}\left(A\right)$. This differential form was later designated as the Chern-Simons form $\mathbf{CS}_{2n-1}\left(A\right)$. It is worth noting that the primitive of this difference in \eqref{eq: difference of invariant polynomials} provides the local expression of secondary characteristic class\,\cite{chern1974characteristic}. Secondary characteristic classes arise as cohomology classes measuring the difference between two trivializations of primary characteristic classes. These classes live in cohomology groups of degree $2k-1$, instead of $2k$, and encode finer topological and geometrical data. 
\par Chern-Simons theory has had a profound impact on topology by catalyzing the development of new 3-dimensional manifold invariants. Simultaneously, it has significantly influenced mathematical physics by motivating the study of moduli spaces and the quantization of geometrical structures. Most importantly, Chern-Simons theory plays a crucial role as the paradigmatic example of a topological quantum field theory distinguished by its rich interior structure and high computability, where we are going to provide a comprehensive discussion in this paper. Section 2 and section 3 will present a systematic and rigorous analysis of the intricate mathematics in Chern-Simons theory.
\par One of the significant applications of Chern-Simons theory in physics is the topologically massive gauge theory.  Indeed, already in the mid-20th century, physicists began to explore potential strategies of introducing mass terms into Lagrangeans in gauge field theory, in order to provide a theoretical interpretation to the mass of particles the field operator describes. It was found that in 4-dimensional case, we cannot introduce a proper mass term into the Lagrangean and preserve the gauge invariance at the same time. For instance, the Lagrangean of electromagnetic field is
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \mathcal{L}\,=\,-\frac{1}{4}\mathbf{F}_{\mu\nu}\mathbf{F}^{\mu\nu},
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
with a gauge invariance under local $U\left(1\right)$ transformation $A_{\rho}\rightarrow A_{\rho}+\partial_{\rho}\Lambda\left(\Vec{x}\right)$. If we impose a Proca mass term to this Lagrangean by hand, then we have
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \mathcal{L}_{Proca}\,=\,-\frac{1}{4}\mathbf{F}_{\mu\nu}\mathbf{F}^{\mu\nu}+\frac{1}{2}m^{2}A_{\rho}A^{\rho}.
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
However,
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \left(A_{\rho}+\partial_{\rho}\Lambda\right)\left(A^{\rho}+\partial^{\rho}\Lambda\right)\,\neq\,A_{\rho}A^{\rho},
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
which means the $U\left(1\right)$ local gauge symmetry of the previous Lagrangean is explicitly broken. In the 1980s, physicists found that in 3-dimensional case, there does exist a feasible mass term that can be introduced without breaking the gauge invariance\,\cite{deser1982three}. The mass term they introduced is an Abelian Chern-Simons term
\begin{equation}\label{Chern-Simons mass term}
    \begin{split}
        \mathcal{L}_{CS}\,=\,\frac{m}{2}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho}A_{\mu}\partial_{\nu}A_{\rho}.
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
When applying a $U\left(1\right)$ gauge transformation on it, we have
\begin{equation}\label{Prove gauge invariance of Chern-Simons term}
    \begin{split}
        \mathcal{L}_{CS}'\,&=\,\frac{m}{2}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho}A_{\mu}'\partial_{\nu}A_{\rho}' \\
        &=\,\frac{m}{2}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho}\left(A_{\mu}+\partial_{\mu}\Lambda\right)\partial_{\nu}\left(A_{\rho}+\partial_{\rho}\Lambda\right) \\
        &=\,\mathcal{L}_{CS}+\frac{m}{2}\epsilon^{\mu\nu\rho}\left[A_{\mu}\left(\partial_{\nu}\partial_{\rho}\Lambda\right)\,+\,\left(\partial_{\mu}\Lambda\right)\left(\partial_{\nu}A_{\rho}\right)\,+\,\left(\partial_{\mu}\Lambda\right)\left(\partial_{\nu}\partial_{\rho}\Lambda\right)\right].
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
Thus, this mass term \eqref{Chern-Simons mass term} can preserve the local gauge symmetry. Originally, the 3-dimensional gauge field with $U\left(1\right)$ symmetry has no dynamics of its own. With adding this term \eqref{Chern-Simons mass term} to the previous Lagrangean $\mathcal{L}_{U\left(1\right)}$, it acquires a propagating degree of freedom corresponding to a massive mode with a single polarization whose direction of rotation is determined by the sign of mass.
