\section{Mathematical Foundations}\label{sec: Mathematical Foundations}
\par This section aims to provide readers with an introduction to the fundamental mathematics which is necessary for understanding the Chern-Simons theory we will discuss in section \ref{sec: Chern-Simons Theory}. The introduction in this section will cover topics ranging from basic concepts in differential geometry to the key mathematical structures in the background of Chern-Simons theory, including the characteristic classes and Chern-Simons form. 
\par We hope this mathematical background would be beneficial to readers, especially to those who are in the physics community and do not have sufficient knowledge of differential geometry. For readers already familiar with basic notions of differential geometry, it might be appropriate to skip some parts of this section and proceed directly to the discussion on characteristic classes and Chern-Weil theory. 
\par It should be noted that in order to understand the material developed in section \ref{sec: Mathematical Foundations}, section \ref{sec: Chern-Simons Theory} and section \ref{sec: C-S Theory in TQFT}, readers are assumed to be familiar with basic knowledge of Lie groups and Lie algebras, including but not limited to the Cartan classification of simple Lie algebras over $\mathbb{C}$ and the adjoint representation for Lie groups. We will not specifically review the theory of Lie groups and Lie algebras in this paper because they play a rudimentary and ubiquitous role in quantum field theory and most physicists are already familiar with them. However, for readers do not have knowledge of group theory, we recommend them to prepare fundamental knowledge with these standard texts \cite{Hall2015},\,\cite{Knapp2002},\,\cite{FultonHarris1991},\,\cite{Humphreys1972} prior to reading the rest of this paper.  
\subsection{Differential Geometry and Fiber Bundles}\label{subsec: Differential Geometry and Fiber Bundles} % 6 pages
\subsubsection{Foundations of Differential Geometry}
\par It is well known that a topology $\mathrm{T}$ on a nonempty set $X$ is a collection of subsets of that set $X$, such that: (i) both the empty set $\emptyset$ and $X$ itself are elements in $\mathrm{T}$; (ii) for any two subsets of $X$ which are elements in $\mathrm{T}$, their intersection is also an element of $\mathrm{T}$; (iii) the union of any collection of sets from $\mathrm{T}$ is also an element of $\mathrm{T}$. A topological space is defined to be a nonempty set with such a given topology $\mathrm{T}$ on it. Based on this simple definition, the so-called open sets in a topological space are just the sets in $\mathrm{T}$, and the closed sets are just those which are not in $\mathrm{T}$. 
\par Suppose $M$ is already a topological space with a topology $\mathrm{T}$, then it is called a topological manifold if and only if it satisfies the following conditions.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
    \item \textbf{Hausdorff property:} For arbitrary $\mathbf{x}_{1},\,\mathbf{x}_{2}\in M$, if $\mathbf{x}_{1}\neq\mathbf{x}_{2}$, then there exists $\mathcal{O}_{1},\,\mathcal{O}_{2}\in\mathrm{T}$, $\mathcal{O}_1\neq\mathcal{O}_{2}$, such that $\mathbf{x}_{1}\in\mathcal{O}_{1}$ and $\mathbf{x}_{2}\in\mathcal{O}_{2}$.
    \item \textbf{Second countability:} For the topology $\mathrm{T}$, there exists a countable basis $\mathcal{B}=\{U_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}$, such that any set from $\mathrm{T}$ is the union of some of these $U_{i}$.
    \item \textbf{Local Euclidean property:} For arbitrary $\mathbf{x}\in M$, there exists a homeomorphism $\phi:U\rightarrow V$, in which $U$ is an open neighborhood of $\mathbf{x}$ and $V$ is an open set in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$.
\end{enumerate}
Topological manifold is a fundamental notion in differential geometry. It has broad applications in theoretical physics, notably in general relativity and topological quantum field theory.
\par A chart on a topological manifold $M$ is defined to be an ordered pair $\left(U,\phi\right)$, where $U$ is an open set on $M$ and $\phi:U\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}$ is a homeomorphism. Then we call $\mathcal{A}=\{\left(U_{\alpha},\phi_{\alpha}\right)\}$ an atlas on $M$ if and only if it satisfies: (i) each $\left(U_{\alpha},\phi_{\alpha}\right)\in\mathcal{A}$ is a chart on $M$; (ii) the union of all these $U_{\alpha}$ in $\mathcal{A}$ could cover the entire topological manifold $M$. It should be noted that, although the most general definition in pure mathematics allows an atlas to contain uncountably many charts, it is customary to assume that only a countable charts exist in each atlas, in order to ensure that the manifold is second-countable. This property of second countability is required in most physical applications. Accordingly, we will restrict the amount of charts in a feasible atlas to be countable in this paper.
\par In physics contexts, it is customary to consider only atlases that are differentiable. A differentiable atlas is an atlas in which for arbitrary two charts $\left(U_{\alpha},\phi_{\alpha}\right)$ and $\left(U_{\beta},\phi_{\beta}\right)$ in it, if $U_{\alpha}\cap U_{\beta}\neq\emptyset$, then their transition function $\phi_{\beta}\circ\phi_{\alpha}^{-1}:\,\phi_{\alpha}\left(U_{\alpha}\cap U_{\beta}\right)\rightarrow\phi_{\beta}\left(U_{\alpha}\cap U_{\beta}\right)$ is smooth, that is, infinitely differentiable $\left(C^{\infty}\right)$. 
\par Suppose that $M$ is a topological manifold with a differentiable atlas $\mathcal{A}$. This differentiable atlas $\mathcal{A}$ is termed a maximum differentiable atlas if and only if it satisfies the following requirement: for any chart $\left(U,\phi\right)$, once the transition functions between it and arbitrary charts in $\mathcal{A}$ are infinitely differentiable, this chart $\left(U,\phi\right)$ must be already contained in $\mathcal{A}$. This means that there does not exist any additional chart that can be added into $\mathcal{A}$ without violating its previous structure of differential operator. The definition of a maximum differentiable atlas is formulated based on the idea of completeness of mathematical structure. A maximum differentiable atlas of a manifold is more commonly referred to as a differentiable structure. 
\par A topological manifold $M$ equipped with a differentiable structure is termed a differentiable manifold. The differentiable manifold is often denoted as $\left(M,\mathfrak{F}_{M}\right)$, where $\mathfrak{F}_{M}$ refers to the given differentiable structure of this differentiable manifold. 
\par There are several crucial types of maps between differentiable manifolds that we have to clarify before introducing the notion of fiber bundle. For a differentiable manifold, we customarily term the local coordinate functions $\phi_{\alpha}\left(\mathbf{x}\right)$ $\left(\forall\,\mathbf{x}\in U_{\alpha}\subset M\right)$ provided by charts in the differentiable structure a local coordinate system. A smooth map between two differentiable manifolds $\left(M,\mathfrak{F}_{M}\right)$ and $\left(N,\mathfrak{F}_{N}\right)$ is a function $f:M\rightarrow N$ whose expressions in local coordinate system are smooth. This type of maps is very important since the fiber bundle projection that will soon be discussed is exactly a smooth map. Building on this, a map is called a diffeomorphism if it is a bijective function, and both itself and its inverse satisfy the above definition of smooth maps. It should be noted that a diffeomorphism is an isomorphism in the category of smooth manifolds. 
\par In order to discuss the notions of tangent space and tangent bundle, we now introduce the definition of tangent vectors at a point on the differentiable manifold first. For a differentiable manifold $\left(M,\mathfrak{F}_{M}\right)$ and a point $p$ on $M$, consider all smooth curves $\gamma: \left(-\epsilon,\epsilon\right)\rightarrow M$ such that $\gamma\left(0\right)=p$. Two such smooth curves $\gamma_{i}$ and $\gamma_{j}$ are viewed as equivalent if and only if there exists a local chart $\left(U_{\alpha},\phi_{\alpha}\right)$ such that the values of derivatives of functions $\phi_{\alpha}\circ\gamma_{i}$ and $\phi_{\alpha}\circ\gamma_{j}$ are equal at $0$. Each class of such equivalent curves is termed a tangent vector at point $p$. 
\par Each point $p$ on a differentiable manifold is associated with a tangent space $T_{p}M$. This tangent space is just a linear space composed of all distinct tangent vectors of the manifold $\left(M,\mathfrak{F}_{M}\right)$ at point $p$. While the tangent space is defined at each single point of the manifold, the notion of tangent bundle is formulated based on the entire differentiable manifold, which means there exists a unique tangent bundle for each given manifold $\left(M,\mathfrak{F}_{M}\right)$. The tangent bundle of a manifold can be simply defined as:
\begin{equation}\label{definition of a tangent bundle}
    \begin{split}
        TM\,:=\,\bigsqcup_{p\in M} T_{p}M.
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
The symbol $\bigsqcup$ is used here to refer to a disjoint union of tangent spaces at all points on $M$, in order to suggest that tangent vectors from different tangent spaces (i.e. tangent spaces of different points) are always regarded as distinct mathematical objects in the entire tangent bundle. The tangent bundle itself is a new differentiable manifold different from the original differentiable manifold $M$. Since the base manifold $M$ has a dimension of $n$ and each tangent space $T_{p}M$ also has a dimension of $n$, the tangent bundle defined as \eqref{definition of a tangent bundle} is a differentiable manifold with dimension $2n$.
\par Suppose $f:M\rightarrow N$ is a smooth map between two differentiable manifolds $M$ and $N$, $p$ is a point on $M$ and $f\left(p\right)=q$. The differential of map $f$ at point $p$ is a linear map which assigns a unique tangent vector in $T_{q}N$ to each tangent vector in $T_{p}M$:
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \mathrm{d}f_{p}:T_{p}M\rightarrow T_{q}N.
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
\par Customarily, there are several equivalent ways to formulate the definition of this differential $\mathrm{d}f_{p}$. We only expound the most common one here, which is from the perspective of classes of equivalent curves we just mentioned. Since each tangent vector is a class of equivalent curves, for each tangent vector, select one arbitrary curve from that corresponding class. Obviously, $f\circ\gamma$ is a feasible curve at point $q\in N$ (i.e. $f\circ\gamma:\,\left(-\epsilon,\epsilon\right)\rightarrow N$, $[f\circ\gamma]\left(0\right)=q$). The differential $\mathrm{d}f_{p}$ is defined to assign the tangent vector associated with the class of curves equivalent with $f\circ\gamma$ to the tangent vector associated with the class of curves equivalent with $\gamma$. 
\par It can be proved that this definition is self-consistent. This is because, once two curves $\gamma_{i}$ and $\gamma_{j}$ on manifold $M$ are equivalent, we have
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}\left(\phi_{\alpha}\circ\gamma_{i}\right)\right|_{\mu=0}\,=\,\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}\left(\phi_{\alpha}\circ\gamma_{j}\right)\right|_{\mu=0},
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
where $\phi_{\alpha}$ is the local coordinate function in chart $\left(U_{\alpha},\phi_{\alpha}\right)$ associated with the open set $U_{\alpha}$ containing point $p$, and $\mu\in\left(-\epsilon,\epsilon\right)$. According to the chain rule and the differentiability of coordinate functions, it follows directly that
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}\left(\psi_{\beta}\circ f\circ\gamma_{i}\right)\right|_{\mu=0}\,=\,\left.\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}\mu}\left(\psi_{\beta}\circ f\circ\gamma_{j}\right)\right|_{\mu=0},
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
in which $\psi_{\beta}$ is the local coordinate function in chart $\left(V_{\beta},\psi_{\beta}\right)$ on manifold $N$ associated with the open set $V_{\beta}$ containing point $q$. Thus, curves $f\circ\gamma_{i}$ and $f\circ\gamma_{j}$ are also equivalent on manifold $N$. This property ensures the well-definedness of this version of definition. 
\par Let $f:M\rightarrow N$ be a smooth map from differentiable manifold $M$ to differentiable manifold $N$. We term $f$ an immersion if and only if its differential $\mathrm{d}f_{p}:T_{p}M\rightarrow T_{f\left(p\right)}N$ at arbitrary point $p\in M$ is an injective map. If the differential of a smooth map $f$ is a surjective map at arbitrary point on $M$, then this map $f$ is called a submersion. Now given that the smooth map $f:M\rightarrow N$ is an immersion, then such $f$ is called an embedding if and only if $f$ is a topological homeomorphism from manifold $M$ to manifold $f\left(M\right)$. Accordingly, the notion of embedding refers to those maps which smoothly and without distortion insert a differentiable manifold into another differentiable manifold, with simultaneously preserving both its differential structure and topological structure.

\subsubsection{Definition And Properties of Fiber Bundles}
\par Fiber bundle is a fundamental notion that lies at a basic level of modern differential geometry and provides a fertile ground for the emergence of Chern-Simons theory. We are going to provide a formal introduction to the notion of fiber bundle in the remainder of this subsection \ref{subsec: Differential Geometry and Fiber Bundles}. 
\par A fiber bundle is a quadruple $\left(E,\pi,M,V\right)$, where $E$, $M$, $V$ are topological spaces and $\pi:E\rightarrow M$ is a surjective map. It should be noted that in the context of Chern-Simons theory and topological quantum field theory, we always exclusively consider the smooth fiber bundles. In this case, all $E$, $M$, $V$ are required to be smooth differentiable manifolds, not only topological spaces, and $\pi$ should be a smooth surjective map. In the quadruple $\left(E,\pi,M,V\right)$ for a feasible fiber bundle, $E$ is its total space, $M$ is its base space, $V$ is its typical fiber, and $\pi:E\rightarrow M$ is its surjective projection map.
\par Such a quadruple $\left(E,\pi,M,V\right)$ is a fiber bundle if and only if it satisfies that: for each arbitrary point $p$ in the base space $M$, there must exist an open neighborhood $W_{\alpha}$ (i.e. $p\in W_{\alpha}$, $W_{\alpha}\subset M$) and a corresponding homeomorphism $\varphi_{\alpha}:\pi^{-1}\left(W_{\alpha}\right)\rightarrow W_{\alpha}\times V$, such that the diagram involving $proj_{1}\circ\varphi_{\alpha}$ and $\pi$ commutes (i.e. $\forall q\in W_{\alpha},\,\forall r\in\pi^{-1}\left(q\right),\,proj_{1}\circ\varphi_{\alpha}\left(r\right)\,=\,\pi\left(r\right)\,=\,q$). Note that the operator $proj_{1}$ here denotes the canonical projection onto the first factor (i.e. $proj_{1}:X\times Y\rightarrow X,\:\left(x,y\right)\mapsto x$).  A feasible ordered pair $\left(W_{\alpha},\varphi_{\alpha}\right)$ satisfying the above properties is customarily termed a local trivialization. The collection of local trivialization near all points on the base space $M$, $\{\left(W_{\alpha},\varphi_{\alpha}\right)\}$, is known as the trivialization atlas of this fiber bundle $\left(E,\pi,M,V\right)$. 
\par An important concept in the theory of fiber bundles is the fiber at each point in the base space $M$, which is commonly denoted as $F_{p}$ ($p\in M$). The fiber at point $p$ is defined as 
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        F_{p}\,:=\,\pi^{-1}\left(p\right)\,=\,\{x\,|\,x\in E,\,\pi\left(x\right)=p\}.
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
\par Obviously, the fiber $F_{p}$ at each point $p\in M$ is endowed with a subtopology inherited from $E$ since $F_{p}$ is an inverse image of the smooth surjective map $\pi$ at $p$. Moreover, both $E$ and $M$ are differentiable manifolds and there exists a local trivialization $\varphi_{\alpha}$ (for which the point $p$ is contained in the open set $W_{\alpha}$ associated with $\varphi_{\alpha}$) which is a smooth homeomorphism (i.e. a diffeomorphism) such that $\varphi_{\alpha}\left(F_{p}\right)\,=\,\{p\}\times V$. Note that $\{p\}\times V$ is an embedded manifold of $W_{\alpha}\times V$. This indicates that $F_{p}$ is an embedded submanifold of $W_{\alpha}\times V$, which is diffeomorphic with the inverse image $\pi^{-1}\left(W_{\alpha}\right)$ that is again an embedded submanifold of $E$. Therefore, $F_{p}$ is an embedded submanifold of the total space $E$. The key point that guarantees the above process is the property of diffeomorphism of the local trivialization that always preserve the differentiable structures of manifolds.
\par We now provide a brief analysis about the dimension of the fiber at each point of the base space. Note that the local trivialization $\varphi_{\alpha}$ is a diffeomorphism and
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \varphi_{\alpha}[\pi^{-1}\left(W_{\alpha}\right)]=W_{\alpha}\times V,
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
thus 
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \dim\,\pi^{-1}\left(W_{\alpha}\right)\,=\,\dim{W_{\alpha}}+\dim{V}.
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
Since $F_{p}$ is diffeomorphic with $V$ and $p\in W_{\alpha}$, we have
\begin{equation}\label{dimension of fiber at each point}
    \begin{split}
        \dim{F_{p}}\,&=\,\dim{V}  \\
        &=\,\dim{\pi^{-1}\left(W_{\alpha}\right)}\,-\,\dim{W_{\alpha}}  \\
        &=\,\dim{E}\,-\,\dim{M}.
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
This derivation \eqref{dimension of fiber at each point} indicates that the dimension of a fiber at each point is equal to the dimension of total space minus the dimension of base space. This is a significant result that should be kept in mind during the following exploration of Chern-Simons theory.
\par In order to establish a solid preparation for the understanding of Chern-Simons theory, it is necessary for us to provide an introduction to the notion of structure group on a fiber bundle. Suppose $\left(E,\pi,M,V\right)$ is a smooth fiber bundle, which follows naturally that $V$ must be a smooth manifold. We define
\begin{equation}\label{define Diff(V)}
    \begin{split}
        \mathrm{Diff}\left(V\right)\,:=\,\{\Psi\,|\,\Psi:V\rightarrow V \,\mathrm{is\,a\,diffeomorphism}\}.
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
It is noteworthy that $\mathrm{Diff}\left(V\right)$ defined by \eqref{define Diff(V)} can be regarded as an infinite-dimensional Lie group whose corresponding Lie algebra is the collection of all smooth vector fields $\mathfrak{X}\left(V\right)$ on the differentiable manifold $V$ and the Lie bracket (commutator) is the same as the Lie bracket of vector fields. Let $\{\left(W_{\alpha},\varphi_{\alpha}\right)\}$ be a trivialization atlas of the fiber bundle $\left(E,\pi,M,V\right)$. A transition function on the overlap $W_{\alpha\beta}$ of two local trivializations $\left(W_{\alpha},\varphi_{\alpha}\right)$ and $\left(W_{\beta},\varphi_{\beta}\right)$ is defined as a function $t_{\alpha\beta}$ that satisfies:
\begin{equation}\label{define transition functions}
    \begin{split}
        &t_{\alpha\beta}:W_{\alpha\beta}\rightarrow \mathrm{Diff}\left(V\right), \\
        &\varphi_{\beta}\circ\varphi_{\alpha}^{-1}\left(p,f\right)\,=\,\left(\,p,t_{\alpha\beta}\left(p\right)[f]\,\right).
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
We now state the definition of the central notion of a structure group of this fiber bundle. The structure group is customarily denoted as $G$. It must be a Lie subgroup of $\mathrm{Diff}\left(V\right)$ such that the images of all points $p$ in the base space under all feasible transition functions $t_{\alpha\beta}$ with $p\in W_{\alpha\beta}$ are contained in it (i.e. $\forall\,\left(W_{\alpha},\varphi_{\alpha}\right),\left(W_{\beta},\varphi_{\beta}\right),\,\mathrm{if}\,W_{\alpha}\cap W_{\beta}=W_{\alpha\beta}\neq\emptyset,\,\forall p\in W_{\alpha\beta},\,t_{\alpha\beta}\left(p\right)\in G$). According to the definition, transition functions of a fiber bundle naturally satisfy that: for an arbitrary point $p$ in the base space $M$,
\begin{equation}\label{cocycle condition}
    \begin{split}
        &t_{\alpha\alpha}\left(p\right)\,=\,\mathrm{id}_{V}, \\
        &t_{\alpha\beta}\left(p\right)\,=\,t_{\beta\alpha}\left(p\right)^{-1}, \\
        &t_{\alpha\beta}\left(p\right)\circ t_{\beta\gamma}\left(p\right)\circ t_{\gamma\alpha}\left(p\right)\,=\,\mathrm{id}_{V},
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
where the notation $\mathrm{id}_{V}\in\mathrm{Diff}\left(V\right)$ denotes the identity map that preserves each input fiber unchanged. This is called the cocycle condition. 
\par It should be noted that the above definition of a structure group only requires the values of all transition functions $t_{\alpha\beta}$ at each point in their respective domains to be elements in it, but the structure group $G$ would still contain elements other than these values of transition functions. Based on this definition, for a unique fiber bundle, there exists multiple options of structure groups. We can always generate one feasible structure group with the collection of all pointwise values of transition functions $t_{\alpha\beta}\left(p\right)$ in a particular way. Suppose $S\subset GL\left(n,\mathbb{R}\right)$ is the set of all pointwise values, i.e.
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        S\,:=\,\{\,t_{\alpha\beta}\left(p\right)\,|\,\forall p\in W_{\alpha\beta}=W_{\alpha}\cap W_{\beta},\,\forall\alpha,\beta\}.
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
Denote the minimal subgroup of $GL\left(n,\mathbb{R}\right)$ generated by $S$ as $\langle S\rangle$. Its closure $\overline{\langle S\rangle}$ is a close subgroup of $GL\left(n,\mathbb{R}\right)$ which is a Lie group with dimension $n^{2}$. According to the Cartan's closed subgroup theorem, $\overline{\langle S\rangle}$ is necessarily a Lie subgroup whose group operations are smooth. Since this group is the closure of a group generated by $S$, it must satisfy the above condition to be a structure group of the given fiber bundle. The structure group generated by $S$ in this way is customarily called a minimal structure group in the theory of fiber bundle. 
\par When a fiber bundle together with its local trivialization atlas is given, its structure groups can be determined. Conversely, if the fiber bundle is unknown but a feasible group and a typical fiber are given, a corresponding fiber bundle can be constructed with this group as its structure group. A group that can be used to construct an associated bundle must satisfy some requirements. Firstly, it is obvious that this group must be a Lie group. Secondly, all pointwise images of transition functions as elements in this group must satisfy the cocycle condition \eqref{cocycle condition}. Thirdly, the structure of the group must match the structure of the typical fiber. That is, 
\par For an arbitrary point $p\in M$, the fiber at that point $F_{p}$ must be diffeomorphic with the typical fiber $V$ of the fiber bundle. This is a direct consequence of the local triviality of a fiber bundle. However, in general, fiber bundles need not to be globally trivial, which means, a fiber bundle generally does not possess a natural global identification among its each-point fibers. The specific type of fiber bundles which do possess such a natural global identification are termed (globally) trivial bundles. Suppose $\left(E,\pi,M,V\right)$ is an arbitrary fiber bundle, $\left(W_{\alpha},\varphi_{\alpha}\right)$ and $\left(W_{\beta},\varphi_{\beta}\right)$ are two of its local trivializations such that $W_{\alpha}\cap W_{\beta}\neq\emptyset$. Let $W_{\alpha}\cap W_{\beta}=W_{\alpha\beta}$. For a point $p\in W_{\alpha\beta}\subset M$, the composition of the two local trivializations is 
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        \varphi_{\alpha}\circ\varphi_{\beta}^{-1}:\,&\varphi_{\beta}[\pi^{-1}\left(W_{\alpha\beta}\right)]\rightarrow\varphi_{\alpha}[\pi^{-1}\left(W_{\alpha\beta}\right)], \\
        &\left(p,f_{p\beta}\right)\,\longmapsto\,\left(p,f_{p\alpha}\right),
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
where 
\begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
        &f_{px}\in\pi^{-1}\left(p\right)\subset \pi^{-1}\left(W_{\alpha\beta}\right)\subset E, \\
        &f_{p\beta}\,:=\,proj_{2}\circ\varphi_{\beta}\left(f_{px}\right), \\
        &f_{p\alpha}\,:=\,proj_{2}\circ\varphi_{\alpha}\left(f_{px}\right).
    \end{split}
\end{equation}
